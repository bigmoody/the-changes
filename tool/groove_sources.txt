Where the grooves come from.

Every pattern in lib/audio/drum_patterns.dart is a claim about how a
groove is played, and a claim that nobody can check is worth nothing. So
each one is written down here with what it rests on, the way every sample
has its source in sample_sources.txt. Anything added to that file should
be added here too.

A line is: the groove, then what the source says, then where it says it.
Where two sources disagree, both are here and the code says which one it
followed and why.


JAZZ WALTZ (_jazzWaltzBar)

Ride figure, "one, two, and-of-two, three": a skip note after the second
beat and beat three left plain.
  "The basic jazz waltz or 3/4 swing pattern consists of 'one, two,
  and-of-two, three' on the ride cymbal, with the hi-hat on 'two'."
  freedrumlessons.com, basic jazz pattern lesson.

Hi-hat under 2 and 3: a listed variation of the same figure, the other
common way being under beat 2 alone. The app follows 2 and 3, which is
also what its own _backbeats rule gives a three-beat bar, so the waltz
and every other groove close the hat in the same places.
  Same source, which lists "the hi-hat on 'two' and 'three', or on all
  three beats" among the variations.

Snare answers on the "and" of 2 or on 3, now and then rather than every
bar.
  Same source: "adding the snare on the 'and-of-two' or on the
  'and-of-one' and on 'three'."
  soundbrenner.com, rhythm library: jazz waltz, on the drummer playing
  sparsely and implying the pulse rather than hitting every beat.

The bass walks three to the bar.
  soundbrenner.com, same article: the bass may "walk in three", play "a
  more spacious one-feel", or alternate strong downbeats with moving
  quarter notes. The app walks, and the two-feel is reachable by
  changing the bass on its own in the editor.

Other syncopations (the skip reaching past the bar into the next one)
are a quarter of bars rather than every bar.
  Same article: other patterns "lift the 'and' of 2, or beats 2 and 3,
  or different syncopations depending on the tune", given as options and
  not as a fixed pattern.

Feathered kick: soft enough to be felt and not heard, never struck hard.
  The general rule for the swing kick, carried over: the bass drum is
  played "only loud enough to be felt, but not heard".


BALLAD, WITH BRUSHES (_balladBrushBar)

The left hand circles the head without stopping, half a circle to a
beat, and the change of direction marks the pulse.
  "THE LEFT HAND stirs in a clockwise circle. There should be a slight
  change in sound as the brush changes directions at the top and bottom
  of the circle, this helps mark the quarter note pulse."
  Nebraska Music Education Association all-state jazz drum set packet.
  percussion.byu.edu, brushes page: start the left hand at nine o'clock
  and swap hands with the right on beats 2 and 4; for ballads, stir the
  hands in opposite directions towards the inside of the drum.

The right hand does not keep time: it touches the head on 2 and 4, where
the hands cross.
  percussion.byu.edu, same page, on switching places on beats 2 and 4.
  The Nebraska packet's medium-swing brush pattern taps the "2 a" and
  "4 a" instead; that is the quicker tempo's version of the same hand,
  and at a ballad the app plays it on the beat.

Direction is the player's own choice, so nothing here depends on it.
  Steve Fidyk, Modern Drummer brush workshop: "Some people naturally
  move counterclockwise, and some people are more comfortable moving
  clockwise."

Nobody comments: no bombs, no fills, no snare answers. This one is the
app's own decision rather than a quotation, and it is in the tests so
that it stays deliberate.


BALLAD, WITH STICKS (_balladBar)

Quarters on the ride with one skip note at the end of the bar, the
hi-hat under 2 and 4, and the same feathered kick. The brush kit is what
a ballad is normally played with; this is the same part for a player who
has chosen sticks, thinned out to match.

SECOND LINE (_secondLineBar)

The accent skeleton: "1, the a of 1, the and of 2, 3, the a of 3, the
and of 4".
  soundbrenner.com, rhythm library: second line. Given as a practice
  pattern for the whole groove rather than for one drum.

Bass drum on the downbeats and the off-beat pickups: 1, the "and" of 2,
3, the "and" of 4.
  The same article: low sounds on 1 and 3, then one moved to the "and"
  of 4 as "an offbeat pickup into the next bar".
  A second source describes the street beat as the downbeat plus the
  syncopated "and" of 2 and "and" of 4, which is the same figure read
  the other way round. The app plays both halves: downbeats on 1 and 3,
  pickups after each backbeat.

Snare: the backbeat on 2 and 4 with ghost notes around it, and
"syncopation, rolls, buzzes, and street-beat vocabulary" besides. The
app plays the backbeat and the ghosts, and an extra crack off the beat
now and then; it has no press roll to play.

No cymbal at all. A brass band is a bass drum and a snare, and that is
what the kit part stands in for. This is the app's own reading of the
sources rather than a quotation: none of them puts a ride in it.

Swung, but not squarely: "often between swung eighths and loose
sixteenths", less triplet-based than a shuffle and less smooth than
swing. The app swings it on the same curve as everything else, which
the Swing slider then moves.

Tempo: "slower processional feels" up to "bright, fast street tempos".

Bass: the tuba or bass "outlines bass motion and reinforces the walking
feel", so it walks.


GYPSY JAZZ, LA POMPE (PianoStyle.laPompe)

Four staccato downstrokes a bar, with the accent on 2 and 4.
  premierguitar.com, "Rhythm Rules: Gypsy Jazz Primer": the most basic
  pattern is four staccato downstrokes per measure with a slight
  emphasis on beats two and four. The same source notes beginners
  mistake it for a two-beat because of that accent.

The accent comes from the arm, not from a harder pick: for beat 2 "the
hand lifts high and drops", using gravity rather than muscle.

The left hand mutes immediately after the stroke, which is where the
drum-like sound comes from, and why the app's strokes are so short.

The quick upstroke before 1 and 3 is a grace note: "its time value is
not relative to the overall tempo of the song. It is played very
quickly no matter what the tempo is." So the app writes it twenty-five
milliseconds before the stroke it leads into, worked back into beats at
whatever tempo the tune is going at, and each bar carries the lift into
the next, which is where it sounds. Written as a fixed fraction of a
beat instead, it dragged further off the beat the slower the tune got.
  Disputed, and recorded as disputed: players on the DjangoBooks forum
  differ over whether the upstrokes are part of the pattern at all, one
  calling it "an effect that you can do, but don't have to". What is
  agreed is that the main beats are always downstrokes, which is what
  the app plays; the upstrokes are quiet enough to be the grace notes
  the primer describes.

Tight, and on small grips. The chop is three or four strings, not six:
there is a bassist for the bottom, and a stroke that has to be in and
out inside a fraction of a beat cannot be six strings wide without the
low ones arriving late. The app's comping hand wanders a little on
purpose, which is what stops a guitar part sounding like a sampler;
this one does not, because in a band with no drummer it is the time.
That is the app's own reading, and it is what [strokeFeel] says.

No drummer. The Quintette du Hot Club de France had none, and "the
guitars and bass had to provide both the harmony and the rhythmic
drive"; the rhythm guitar's "short, percussive attack" does what a kit
otherwise would.
  en.wikipedia.org, Gypsy jazz.
  allaboutjazz.com, Clément Réboul interview, on the bass as "the base
  of the groove" locked to la pompe.

Bass: both a two-feel and walking four are standard, and players mix
them. The app walks and leaves the two-feel a dropdown away.
  Soundslice's gypsy jazz bass course teaches both on the same tunes.

AFRO 6/8 (_afroSixBar)

The name first. The groove jazz bands play is an approximation of Cuban
folkloric music, and a drummer who teaches it says plainly that Bembé
"is religious ceremonial music, and is highly specific in every aspect;
it's not really appropriate to refer to the groove/style used by US
jazz musicians by that name. So I say Afro 6, or Afro-Cuban 6/8."
  cruiseshipdrummer.com. The app says Afro 6/8.

The bell: seven strokes across twelve eighths, on the first, third,
fifth, sixth, eighth, tenth and twelfth.
  Jeff Lindqvist / Language Log's clock model of the standard pattern
  places the strikes at twelve, two, four, five, seven, nine and eleven
  on a twelve-hour face, which is the same seven positions.
  cruiseshipdrummer.com, on the same pattern: it is "the familiar 12/8
  or 6/8 bell", written the same way in 3/4, 6/8 or 12/8 with nothing
  changed but the bar lines. That is why the app keeps it as a cycle of
  eighths and lays it over whatever bar it meets: in 12/8 one bar, in
  6/8 two, on a tune in four the same seven strokes over triplets.

The bell can be played on a cowbell, a ride, or the rim of a floor tom;
the app plays it on the cowbell it has.
  Nebraska Music Education Association all-state drum set packet, which
  gives an Afro-Cuban 6/8 feel with the bell, a hi-hat with the foot
  and an optional cross-stick on the snare. The app plays the
  cross-stick now and then rather than every time round.

The bass drum: "the bass drum note on 2 corresponds to the 'bombo' in
Cuban music", so it is on the second beat of the four-pulse cycle and
nowhere else.
  cruiseshipdrummer.com.

The hi-hat is the one thing the sources would not settle. The same
writer warns that "the waltzy hihat part is not" Cuban practice, while
the Nebraska packet has a foot part without spelling it out. So the app
keeps the foot to the two halves of the cycle, out of the way of the
bell, and that choice is the app's own.

The shaker: every one of the twelve pulses, the bell's own strokes
among them struck harder. In the ensemble this is the axatse, the
gourd rattle, and it is what fills the groove in: its down strokes
"sound the standard pattern" by striking the gourd against the knee
and its up strokes "sound pulses in between the bell strokes". One
description calls it "the eighth note version of what the gankogui
plays".
  en.wikipedia.org, Ewe music. The hi-hat is standing in for a gourd,
  which is the app's own substitution and is why it is quiet.

The same article gives the bell's positions in another notation, as a
check on the ones above: 1, 1a, 2& 2a, 3&, 4, 4a of a twelve-pulse
bar, which is the same seven eighths.

The feet: a steady pulse rather than every other one. "Both feet hold
down the groove by playing a steady dotted quarter note pattern", with
splashing the hi-hat every other dotted quarter given as a variation
rather than the base.
  rhythmnotes.net, Afro-Cuban rhythms for drum set.

The second kick: the bombo on the second beat of the cycle, and the
one on the fourth most times round rather than every time, because the
same source calls the kick "on beat three" optional, "especially if
the groove is too heavy for the song".

The congas, from a four-drum bembé written in block notation against
the twelve pulses, "1 & a 2 & a | 3 & a 4 & a":

  Qui:  | O S - O S - | O S - O S - |
  Seg:  | - - - - O O | - - - - O O |
  Tum:  | - - S - - - | - - O - - - |
  SupT: | O - - - - - | - - - - - - |

  mrcrowder.us, hand drumming block notation, "Bembe on 4 drums".
  O is an open tone and S a slap.

On a kit those voices go where the drum-set sources put them: "the
toms on the drum set can imitate some of the basic conga parts", with
the cross-stick as the slap and the toms as the open tones.
  rhythmnotes.net, Afro-Cuban rhythms for drum set.

So the segundo's two pairs of open tones are the high tom, the tumba's
slap is the cross-stick and its open tone the low tom, and the super
tumba's single note is the low tom on the top of the cycle. The second
of each segundo pair leans into the beat after it and is the louder of
the two, which is the app's own reading and not in the notation.

The quinto is the one voice deliberately left out. It plays an open
tone and a slap on every pulse, and under a bell and a shaker that
already sound every pulse it is a third part saying the same thing:
on a kit there are two hands, and the segundo's answer is the one
worth having.

The bass plays roots and fifths on the pulse. A 6/8 tumbao is a line of
its own and is not written yet; this is the plain thing rather than a
guess at one.


GROOVES THAT ARE A FIGURE, IN A BAR THEY DO NOT FIT

Not a source, a rule, written down because it is a musical claim: some
grooves here are written beat by beat and fit any bar (swing, a ballad,
a waltz, and the bell, which is a cycle), while others *are* a
bar-length figure: the bossa clave, the calypso 3-3-2, the samba's two
bars, the funk riff, the second line's street beat. Cut to a bar of
another length those are not themselves but a fragment of themselves.
A calypso counted in six eight came out as half a 3-3-2 with its
off-beats dragged onto triplets, which is nobody's music.

So a groove whose figure takes a bar of four hands over, in any other
bar, to one that fits: the bell in compound time, swing everywhere
else. The whole band goes with it, because a bossa bass under a bell is
two grooves at once, and the mixer says which groove is playing and
why.

SWING IN COMPOUND TIME (_swingBar, _balladBar, compound: true)

A swung bar of six eight or twelve eight takes the skip note on every
beat rather than on the backbeats alone: the first and third of each
triplet, "DA-da, DA-da", long-short all the way through.

  The shuffle ride "often lands on 1 and let, then 2 and let", which
  reads as "DA - da, DA - da" with the accent on the first of each
  triplet, and the feel described as long-short across the beat.
  soundbrenner.com, slow blues shuffle.

  The notation is 12/8, "or equivalently 4/4 with eighth-note
  triplets": one lesson writes its exercises as eighth-note triplets in
  4/4 and says this is basically exactly equivalent to 12/8.

  thedrumninja.com calls the basic blues beat "a triplet based pattern
  in 12/8", with the twelve eighths "usually played on the hi-hat or
  ride cymbal", which is the fuller version: all twelve rather than the
  first and third of each group. bluesharmonica.com separates the two,
  saying that in shuffles and swings "you commonly only hear the first
  and third part of the triplet", while a slow blues often has all of
  them. The app plays the sparse one, which is the jazz reading and
  the one its own swing feel already has in four; the full twelve is a
  slow-blues sound it does not claim to be.

Why it matters here: without it a bar of six eight is the swing bar of
four with its beats renamed, three strokes to a bar and nothing between
them, and it sounds like four four at half speed. That is what it
sounded like.
