Where the grooves come from.

Every pattern in lib/audio/drum_patterns.dart is a claim about how a
groove is played, and a claim that nobody can check is worth nothing. So
each one is written down here with what it rests on, the way every sample
has its source in sample_sources.txt. Anything added to that file should
be added here too.

A line is: the groove, then what the source says, then where it says it.
Where two sources disagree, both are here and the code says which one it
followed and why.


JAZZ WALTZ (_jazzWaltzBar)

Ride figure, "one, two, and-of-two, three": a skip note after the second
beat and beat three left plain.
  "The basic jazz waltz or 3/4 swing pattern consists of 'one, two,
  and-of-two, three' on the ride cymbal, with the hi-hat on 'two'."
  freedrumlessons.com, basic jazz pattern lesson.

Hi-hat under 2 and 3: a listed variation of the same figure, the other
common way being under beat 2 alone. The app follows 2 and 3, which is
also what its own _backbeats rule gives a three-beat bar, so the waltz
and every other groove close the hat in the same places.
  Same source, which lists "the hi-hat on 'two' and 'three', or on all
  three beats" among the variations.

Snare answers on the "and" of 2 or on 3, now and then rather than every
bar.
  Same source: "adding the snare on the 'and-of-two' or on the
  'and-of-one' and on 'three'."
  soundbrenner.com, rhythm library: jazz waltz, on the drummer playing
  sparsely and implying the pulse rather than hitting every beat.

The bass walks three to the bar.
  soundbrenner.com, same article: the bass may "walk in three", play "a
  more spacious one-feel", or alternate strong downbeats with moving
  quarter notes. The app walks, and the two-feel is reachable by
  changing the bass on its own in the editor.

Other syncopations (the skip reaching past the bar into the next one)
are a quarter of bars rather than every bar.
  Same article: other patterns "lift the 'and' of 2, or beats 2 and 3,
  or different syncopations depending on the tune", given as options and
  not as a fixed pattern.

Feathered kick: soft enough to be felt and not heard, never struck hard.
  The general rule for the swing kick, carried over: the bass drum is
  played "only loud enough to be felt, but not heard".


BALLAD, WITH BRUSHES (_balladBrushBar)

The left hand circles the head without stopping, half a circle to a
beat, and the change of direction marks the pulse.
  "THE LEFT HAND stirs in a clockwise circle. There should be a slight
  change in sound as the brush changes directions at the top and bottom
  of the circle, this helps mark the quarter note pulse."
  Nebraska Music Education Association all-state jazz drum set packet.
  percussion.byu.edu, brushes page: start the left hand at nine o'clock
  and swap hands with the right on beats 2 and 4; for ballads, stir the
  hands in opposite directions towards the inside of the drum.

The right hand does not keep time: it touches the head on 2 and 4, where
the hands cross.
  percussion.byu.edu, same page, on switching places on beats 2 and 4.
  The Nebraska packet's medium-swing brush pattern taps the "2 a" and
  "4 a" instead; that is the quicker tempo's version of the same hand,
  and at a ballad the app plays it on the beat.

Direction is the player's own choice, so nothing here depends on it.
  Steve Fidyk, Modern Drummer brush workshop: "Some people naturally
  move counterclockwise, and some people are more comfortable moving
  clockwise."

Nobody comments: no bombs, no fills, no snare answers. This one is the
app's own decision rather than a quotation, and it is in the tests so
that it stays deliberate.


BALLAD, WITH STICKS (_balladBar)

Quarters on the ride with one skip note at the end of the bar, the
hi-hat under 2 and 4, and the same feathered kick. The brush kit is what
a ballad is normally played with; this is the same part for a player who
has chosen sticks, thinned out to match.
